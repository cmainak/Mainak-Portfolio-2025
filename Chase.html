CHASE

Adding a Finance Feature – Surplus Manager

ROLE: Product Design (UX/UI)

TIMELINE: 9 weeks – 80+ hours

TOOLS USED: Figma

This project is a fictitious scenario, completed as a part of passion project. VIEW FINAL PROTOTYPE

Background

CChase Bank serves millions of customers across the United States, providing a robust suite of financial tools through the Chase Mobile® app. Users can check balances, transfer funds, pay bills, deposit checks, review detailed spending reports, manage account alerts, and monitor their credit score with Credit Journey®.

The app also integrates J.P. Morgan investment management and includes features like Autosave, budgeting tools, and customizable notifications. While these capabilities make it convenient for users to manage daily finances securely and efficiently, the app’s current offerings still require customers to initiate most actions themselves.

Problem

More than half of Millennials (58%) and Gen Z (57%) are likely to switch banks if offered better digital experiences or greater personalization. Additionally, 73% of consumers express more interest in financial services from tech giants like Google, Apple, and PayPal than from their current banks.

While Chase offers many advanced digital tools, there is no feature within the app that proactively identifies and highlights surplus cash—meaning the extra funds left in a checking account after routine bills and everyday spending. Without an automated way to spot and move this surplus, younger users may miss valuable opportunities to save or invest.

As expectations for smarter, more proactive digital banking rise, Chase sees an opportunity to attract and retain young professionals by helping them overcome these practical financial challenges with easy-to-use tools built directly into the Chase Mobile® app.

Project Scope

This project focuses on designing a new surplus management feature for the Chase Mobile® app primarily aimed at Millennials and Gen Z users but beneficial to all customers. The feature will help users easily identify surplus cash left in their checking accounts after regular spending and bills, and to move this surplus into savings or investment accounts with minimal effort.

01 / Empathize – Exploring the User’s Needs

Research Approach

To deeply understand the financial habits and pain points of young adults, I conducted exploratory interviews with Chase Mobile® app users aged 25 to 35 who are single, do not own a home, and manage most of their banking digitally. These users were selected because they represent young professionals most likely to seek practical, mobile-first money management solutions.

I recruited five participants for 45- to 60-minute interviews. Discussion focused on how they track spending, plan for savings, and interact with both banking and third-party finance apps. I used open-ended questions to let participants share their unique financial journeys, frustrations, and routines, while also probing for unmet needs and workarounds.

The goals for my research process was the following:

  • Explore and identify patterns in how young professionals manage their finances.
  • Identify pain points and unmet needs that current banks/financial services do not address.
  • Understand what motivates or discourages saving and investing habits among this group

Key Findings from Customer Interviews

Even though participants had different financial goals—from paying off student loans to planning for future big purchases—they all had to juggle finances carefully between paychecks.

Most had tried using apps like Mint, and Rocket Money but quickly became frustrated with inaccurate budget categories, laggy updates, and the need to pay premium fees for additional features just to effectively track their finances.

Two participants resorted to spreadsheets and handwritten logs to manage expenses and track savings goals, finding these more flexible than current app-based solutions.

Many wished payment dates were easier to manage—some even aspired to use autopay, but hesitated due to fear of losing control or overdrawing accounts.

02 / Define – Establishing the User's Needs and Problems

Overview

This stage focuses on synthesizing the research insights into clear problem statements and defining the target persona.

Persona Development

The five participants were all single and did not own a home but varied on financial status, goals, and struggles. Two of the participants were on top of their finances and kept records to keep on track with payments and savings goals. One participant was struggling to pay down her sizable debt for the last 8 years of her life. Another participant did not struggle particularly with debt or strive to reach a financial goal and was content with her financial status as is. The last participant was living paycheck to paycheck but had savings goals and was looking to stabilize her future.

Although the participants varied in financial status, goals, and struggles, they all worked off of their paycheck and did not have other investments or financial advisors. So I created Jasmine as the key persona to focus on for this feature. She is someone who has some debt, savings goals, and wants to have a better grasp on her finances. Like the participants, she manages her finances paycheck to paycheck. She is looking to make a significant commitment to attend graduate school but needs to save more money and stay on top of her finances.

Problem Statements

After conducting the interviews with my participants, I focused my energy on creating “How Might We” problem statements. After composing 18 problem statements, I noticed that there were general recurring themes and specific problems that I was interested in tackling.

These are the 3 problem statements I decided to focus on before the Ideation phase:

  • How might we show users how they are using their money so that they can understand how they are spending their money?
  • How might we educate users to prepare for life goals they are actively looking to achieve?
  • How might we remind users of their finance goals so that they are able to achieve those goals and not go off track?

03 / Ideate – Creating the Framework

Ideation

For the Ideation phase, I utilized the Crazy 8 brainstorming exercise. This exercise involved five-minute intervals of eight sketches followed by short breaks. While I didn’t always complete eight sketches each time, this method pushed me to explore multiple ideas quickly. After five sessions, I compiled and grouped my sketches into key themes.

REMAINING BALANCE MANAGER

SAVINGS GOALS + SHORT/LONG TERM

TRACKING CALENDAR / BILL PAY

EXPENSE BREAKDOWN

ACCOUNT TRACKER / FINANCE EDUCATION

Storyboarding

In this phase, I used the Crazy 8 exercise again to illustrate how a user would interact with a specific idea. After five sessions, I focused on the “Managing by Pay Period” feature. This approach aligned closely with participants’ actual behavior of budgeting per paycheck.

MANAGING INCOME AND EXPENDITURES (2)

MANAGING BY PAY PERIOD (2)

IMPROVING FINANCIAL HEALTH

User Flow

After sketching out these storyboards, I decided to design a feature that helps users manage finances by paycheck period while integrating ways to improve financial health and manage expenses. Initially named “Money Manager,” this feature evolved into the “Surplus Manager” after testing the low-fidelity prototype.

04 / Prototype – Let's Make the Design!

Wireframes

While developing the storyboard concept of paying by paycheck, I examined Bank of America’s mobile banking app and noticed features like “Bill Pay” and “Life Plan” that had been available for years but were largely unnoticed by users. Even participants who were existing customers had never used them.

I decided to integrate similar ideas into my finance management feature. Participants managed finances based on their paycheck, bills, and savings goals, so the new feature would allow them to set up their plan based on pay schedules (bi-weekly, monthly, or custom).

The “Money Manager” feature appears on the dashboard and displays the available balance, scheduled bill payments, and remaining funds until the next paycheck. I also integrated goal tracking and easy access to upcoming payments.

WIREFRAMES

View Low-Fidelity Prototype

Low-Fidelity Prototype Usability Testing

I tested this prototype with 3 participants. Feedback revealed that some UI elements caused confusion, such as large negative balances in “Pending Transactions.” Based on insights, I iterated the flow to prioritize surplus balance management rather than individual payment flows.

High-Fidelity Prototype

Based on user feedback, I renamed the feature from “Money Manager” to “Surplus Manager” to better reflect its purpose. Participants found the previous name misleading as it sounded investment-related.

Key Highlights

  • Surplus Spotlight: Central focus on remaining funds after bills.
  • Manage by Pay Period: Users can track spending across pay cycles.

05 / Test – It’s Time to Test the Prototype!

Usability Test Plan

I conducted tests with 4 participants—2 of whom were part of the low-fidelity prototype testing. For high-fidelity testing, the platform did not allow Bank of America demo logins, so participants used screenshots and scenario-based tasks. I ensured the interface felt intuitive for both Bank of America users and non-users.

Usability Test Findings

Participants appreciated the feature, noting it would simplify financial tracking and make the experience engaging. Key feedback included:

  • “Select Amount” confused users—they thought it referred to paying from the Surplus Balance.
  • One participant assumed “Minimum Amount” meant “total due” rather than minimum payment.
  • The term “Open Balances” was unclear for some participants, but they liked the interactive chart after explanation.
  • Participants suggested integrating Outgoing Payments more closely with outstanding balance management.

Priority Revisions

Based on feedback, I created a new user flow focusing on the Outgoing Payments pathway. This approach offers a more direct route for paying down debt and provides better clarity during transactions.

UPDATED USER FLOW

Next Steps

I plan to test the new Outgoing Payments pathway further. This direct approach addresses user concerns and creates a streamlined experience for paying down credit card debt.
