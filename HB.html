An app that turns daily routines into lasting habits.

By applying behavioral psychology to a gamified garden, I created a system that sustains motivation and helps users build routines that stick.

01. The Problem

Imagine making a promise to yourself to start a fitness routine or learn a new language. The first week goes well. The second week, not so much. Then eventually you lose all hope. 73% of people abandon their habits within the first month.

Despite a saturated market of habit-tracking apps, users continue to struggle with long-term commitment. The problem isn't a lack of tools—it's that existing solutions don't address the psychological reality of motivation decline.

Competitive Audit Findings

All leading apps track—none support. A competitive audit of the three dominant apps revealed the same gap: they share identical feature sets centered on goal tracking, but none address the emotional and psychological challenges users face when motivation drops.

01 Habit App Simple Goal Tracking. Lacks emotional reinforcement.
02 Habitify Goal Tracking + Diary. High friction for daily check-ins.
03 Fabulous Narrative Goal Tracking. Overly structured and rigid for most users.

The Design Challenge

"How might I sustain user motivation through psychological design instead of just tracking behavior?"

02. Strategy & Approach

In my literature review of habit frameworks, I discovered the Hook Model. It's a particularly effective approach for increasing investment in habit formation through a cyclical process of triggers, actions, rewards, and investments. It directly addresses motivation decline by creating multiple reinforcement points throughout the habit cycle.

Design Principles Based on Personas

01 Visual Progress Build stronger motivation through clear visualization of progress. Users need to see tangible results of their efforts.
02 Coherent Integration Minimize distractions and concentration breaks from the user's routine. Respects busy lifestyles.
03 Simple Tracking Busy users should need as little time as possible to use Habit Boost. Reduces friction to habit formation.

03. End-to-End Process

1. Research Interviews & analysis of user motivation drop-offs.
2. Strategy Defined the approach using the Hook Model.
3. Design Architectural flows & low-fidelity wireframing.
4. Testing 5 test rounds to validate ease of use and friction.
5. Delivery Final prototype with validated user impact.

04. Personas & Goals

Consolidating key insights into 2 goal-oriented personas allowed me to balance the need for motivational reflection with the necessity of speed.

Katniss, 22 (Hectic)

Goal: Maintain Long-Lasting Habits

She needs to ensure motivation remains high to stay inspired in completing her routines.

Travis, 26 (Focused)

Goal: Time-Manage Daily Routines

He needs a reliable & simple way to keep up with his to-dos amidst a very busy schedule.

05. The Solution Direction: A Garden Metaphor

These insights led to a cultivation visual metaphor—a garden that grows with commitment but also reflects natural cycles of growth and rest. Unlike traditional point systems that punish failure (streaks), the garden provides the visual progress and compassionate accountability missing from competitor apps.

06. Key Design Decisions

Decision 1: Feature Prioritization for MVP

I prioritized features that required low effort but yielded high impact. I led an initial voting system and cross-discipline workshops, constantly referring back to persona pain points. I made the tough call to say no to social features (which competed with the need for private reflection) and prioritized the core habit-reward loop instead.

Decision 2: Streamlined User Flow

Every additional step increases friction. I designed user flows that minimised time investment while maximising the psychological reward of progress visualization—supporting busy schedules directly.

Decision 3: Usability-Driven Iterations

I led 5 rounds of moderated usability testing with 15 participants over 2 weeks. Testing revealed that initial designs created unnecessary cognitive load.

Iteration 01

Removing Bottom Nav

Why: Testing showed secondary navigation competed with primary actions, contradicting the "Simple" principle. I chose focused hierarchy over feature parity.

Iteration 02

Consolidating Tracking & Progress

Why: Separating habits from progress created cognitive load. Combining them delivered instant gratification aligning with the Hook Model's reward principle.

Iteration 03

Prioritizing Habit Overview

Why: Restructured the home screen to show habit status first, addressing the user's need for motivational feedback before exploring.

07. Core Feature Walkthrough

Creating Good Habits

Personalize your habit or select from a list. Your garden will grow as the commitment does. (Addresses Travis's need for quick setup)

Keeping Up With Your Goals

A familiar and simple way of tracking habits without overwhelming complexity.

Staying Invested and Inspired

Visualizing good progress, growth and success to keep motivation high. (Core to Katniss's sustained motivation needs)

Support for Uninspiring Days

Keeping users accountable and determined to bounce back. (Responds directly to the "cheat day spiral" pattern identified in research)

08. The Final Prototype & Outcomes

The final interactive prototype resulted in a 73% increase in habit completion (11 out of 15 users completed their first habit goal during testing compared to the 4/15 baseline). Navigation simplification reduced required clicks by 67%, driving an 82 SUS Score.

09. Lessons Learned

01 Simplicity vs. Personalization Initial designs overwhelmed users with customization options. I implemented "smart defaults" with progressive disclosure, reducing onboarding from 3+ minutes to 45 seconds.
02 Accessibility Oversight Colorblind users couldn't distinguish habit status by color alone. I redesigned elements using icons + color + text labels to achieve WCAG AA compliance. Testing for accessibility must happen early.
03 Feature Bloat Removed social features after testing showed 78% of users preferred private tracking. User satisfaction increased from 72% to 92%. The best feature is sometimes the one you don't build.
