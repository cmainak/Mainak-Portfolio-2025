Chapter 01 — The Problem

Most people abandon habits
within a month

Imagine making a promise to yourself…

To start a fitness routine, learn a new language, pick up a new craft, or even to drink more water. The first week goes well. The second week, not so much. Then eventually you lose all hope with your new commitment.

73% of people abandon their habits within the first month. Despite a saturated market of habit-tracking apps, users continue to struggle with long-term commitment. The problem isn't a lack of tools it's that existing solutions don't address the psychological reality of motivation decline.

All Leading Apps Track — None Support

The Hook Model Framework

A Cyclical Approach to Sustaining Engagement

In my literature review of habit frameworks, I discovered the Hook Model a particularly effective approach for increasing investment in habit formation through a cyclical process of triggers, actions, rewards, and investments.

Why this framework: The Hook Model directly addresses motivation decline by creating multiple reinforcement points throughout the habit cycle, not just at completion.

User Flow Diagram

3 Design Principles from Research

Translating persona insights into a clear design direction.

Visual

Build stronger motivation through clear visualization of progress. Users need to see tangible results of their efforts.

Coherent

Minimize distractions and concentration breaks from the user's routine. Respects busy lifestyles.

Simple

Busy users should need as little time as possible to use Habit Boost. Reduces friction to habit formation.

The Solution Direction: A Garden Metaphor

These insights led to a cultivation visual metaphor a garden that grows with commitment but also reflects natural cycles of growth and rest. This approach provides the visual progress and compassionate accountability missing from competitor apps.

Why the garden metaphor: Unlike traditional point systems that punish failure, the garden allows for natural growth cycles. When users mentioned feeling "judged" by streak counters, I realized visualization needed to be encouraging, not punitive.

Chapter 03 — Key Decisions

How research insights shaped
my design choices

Personas & Goals

Consolidating key insights into 2 goal-oriented personas.

Hectic

Katniss, 22

Goal: Maintain Long-Lasting Habits

She needs to ensure motivation remains high to stay inspired in completing her routines.

Focused

Travis, 26

Goal: Better Time-Manage Daily Routines

He needs a reliable & simple way to keep up with his to-dos amidst a very busy schedule.

D1

Feature Prioritization for MVP

I prioritized features that required low effort but yielded high impact. I led an initial voting system and cross-discipline workshops, constantly referring back to persona pain points. I made the tough call to say no to social features (which competed with Katniss's need for private reflection) and prioritize the core habit-reward loop instead.

Decision Result: Features divided into 3 release stages through voting, cross-discipline workshops, and constant reference to persona pain points. Every feature decision was justified by user needs.

D2

Streamlined User Flow

Every additional step increases friction. I designed user flows that minimised time investment while maximising the psychological reward of progress visualization supporting Travis's busy schedule directly.

D3

Usability-Driven Iterations

I led 5 rounds of moderated usability testing with 15 participants over 2 weeks. Testing revealed that initial designs created unnecessary cognitive load. I iterated based on specific user feedback that traced back to my core principles of simplicity and coherence.

Iteration 01

Removing Bottom Navigation

Why: Testing showed secondary navigation competed with primary actions, contradicting the "Simple" principle. I chose focused hierarchy over feature parity.

Iteration 02

Consolidating Tracking & Progress

Why: Separating habits from progress created cognitive load. Combining them delivered instant gratification aligning with the Hook Model's reward principle.

Iteration 03

Prioritizing Habit Overview

Why: Users sought reassurance before exploration. Restructured the home screen to show habit status first addressing Katniss's need for motivational feedback.

Chapter 05 — Reflections

Challenges & Learnings

1

Simplicity vs. Personalization

Initial designs overwhelmed users with customization options, contradicting Travis's need for speed. Solution: "Smart defaults" with progressive disclosure. Onboarding reduced from 3+ min to 45 sec.

2

Accessibility Oversight

3 colorblind users couldn't distinguish habit status by color alone. Redesigned using icons + color + text labels, achieving WCAG AA compliance. Learning: Accessibility testing should happen alongside initial usability testing, not after.

3

Late-Stage IA Testing

Navigation confusion discovered in week 5, requiring significant rework of information architecture. Learning: Test IA early with lo-fi prototypes, not after investing in high-fidelity designs.

4

Feature Bloat

Removed social features after testing showed 78% of users preferred private tracking. Validated Katniss's persona needs. User satisfaction increased from 72% to 92%. Learning: The best feature is sometimes the one you don't build.

Key Takeaway

Every design decision should trace back to specific user needs or research insights. When staying true to personas and core principles (Visual, Coherent, Simple), better decisions happen faster.
