THE PROBLEM

Most people abandon habits within a month. Current apps aren't helping

73% of people abandon their habits within the first month. Despite a saturated market of habit-tracking apps, users continue to struggle with long-term commitment. The problem isn't a lack of tools. It's that existing solutions don't address the psychological reality of motivation decline.

To understand why current solutions weren't working, I examined both the competitive landscape and user experiences with existing apps.

All leading apps focus on tracking but ignore the "why" behind giving up

THE HOOK MODEL FRAMEWORK

A cyclical approach to sustaining engagement

In my literature review of habit frameworks, I discovered the Hook Model. This is a particularly effective approach for increasing investment in habit formation through a cyclical process of triggers, actions, rewards, and investments.

Why I chose this framework: The Hook Model directly addresses the motivation decline problem by creating multiple reinforcement points throughout the habit cycle, not just at completion.

DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Translating research insights into design direction

Based on the personas I developed (Katniss's need for sustained motivation and Travis's need for quick, simple engagement), I established three core principles to guide my solution.

VISUAL

Build stronger motivation by providing clear visualization of progress. This addresses users' need to see tangible results of their efforts.

COHERENT

Minimize distractions and concentration breaks from our user's routine. This respects Travis's busy schedule.

SIMPLE

Our busy users should need as little time as possible to use HabitBoost. This reduces friction to habit formation.

THE SOLUTION DIRECTION

A garden metaphor for compassionate habit-building

These insights led me to a cultivation visual metaphor. A garden that grows with commitment but also reflects natural cycles of growth and rest. This approach would provide the visual progress and compassionate accountability missing from competitor apps.

Why I chose the garden metaphor: Unlike traditional point systems that punish failure, the garden allows for natural growth cycles. When users mentioned feeling "judged" by streak counters, I realized visualization needed to be encouraging, not punitive. I hypothesized that this psychological approach would better sustain habits than traditional gamification.

DECISION 1: FEATURE PRIORITIZATION FOR MVP

How I chose high-impact, low-effort features aligned with persona needs

With limited resources and Travis's need for simplicity, I prioritized features that required low effort but yielded high impact. I facilitated an initial voting system and several cross-discipline workshops, constantly referring back to our personas' pain points. This meant I made the tough call to say no to social features (which competed with Katniss's need for private reflection) and prioritize the core habit-reward loop instead.

My Decision Result: I divided features into 3 release stages through voting, cross-discipline workshops, and constant reference to our personas' pain points. This ensured every feature decision was justified by user needs.

DECISION 2: STREAMLINED USER FLOW

Focusing on minimum steps and time to support Travis's busy schedule

Every additional step increases friction. I designed user flows that minimized time investment while maximizing the psychological reward of progress visualization.

DECISION 3: USABILITY-DRIVEN ITERATIONS

How I conducted 5 rounds of testing to refine the experience

I led 5 rounds of moderated usability testing with 15 participants over 2 weeks. Testing revealed that my initial designs created unnecessary cognitive load. I iterated based on specific user feedback that traced back to my core principles of simplicity and coherence.

Removing Bottom Navigation

Why I made this decision: Testing showed secondary navigation competed with primary actions, contradicting my "simple" principle. I chose focused hierarchy over feature parity.

Consolidating Tracking & Progress

Why I made this decision: Separating habits from progress created cognitive load. Combining them delivered instant gratification. This aligned with the Hook Model's reward principle.

Prioritizing Habit Overview

Why I made this decision: Users sought reassurance before exploration. I restructured the home screen to show habit status first, addressing Katniss's need for motivational feedback.

Reflections

CHALLENGES & LEARNINGS

1. Simplicity vs. Personalization: My initial designs overwhelmed users with customization options, contradicting Travis's need for speed. Solution: I implemented "smart defaults" with progressive disclosure. Users start simple, customize later. I reduced onboarding from 3+ min to 45 sec.

2. Accessibility Oversight: 3 colorblind users couldn't distinguish habit status by color alone. I redesigned using icons + color + text labels, achieving WCAG AA compliance and improving clarity for all users. Learning: Accessibility testing should happen alongside initial usability testing, not after.

3. Late-Stage IA Testing: I discovered navigation confusion in week 5, requiring significant rework of my information architecture. Learning: Test information architecture early with lo-fi prototypes, not after investing in high-fidelity designs.

4. Feature Bloat: I removed social features after testing showed 78% of users preferred private tracking. This validated Katniss's persona needs. Result: User satisfaction increased from 72% to 92%. Learning: The best feature is sometimes the one you don't build.

Key Takeaway: Every design decision should trace back to specific user needs or research insights. When I stayed true to my personas and core principles (Visual, Coherent, Simple), I made better decisions faster.
