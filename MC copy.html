PORTFOLIO REDESIGN

This self-initiated project was focused on rebranding and rebuilding my personal UX portfolio from scratch.

Timeline – Dec 2023 to Present

Tools – Figma, HTML, CSS, JS, GitHub, Netlify

Skills -

UX Research Responsive Web Design Bootstrap (Framework) Visual Design

BACKGROUND

When I first began building my portfolio, I did what most designers do: crafted it based on my own tastes, ideas, and assumptions. But halfway through my master’s program, a piece of advice hit me like a reality check:

"Your portfolio is your work, but you are not the user."

That shifted everything. To redesign my portfolio through a user-centered lens, I had to start by understanding the actual users — recruiters and hiring managers — and their needs, expectations, and frustrations with design portfolios.

THE PROBLEM

My portfolio was built around my own preferences and assumptions, not around the actual users — recruiters and hiring managers. This made it harder for them to quickly understand my strengths and for me to stand out in a crowded job market.

🔷 🔷 DOUBLE DIAMOND DESIGN PROCESS

The project follows a user-centered design thinking approach aligned with the Double Diamond model — emphasizing research, collaboration, and iterative validation to meet real recruiter needs.

PHASE 1 🔍 DISCOVER

📋 Research Objectives

  • Understand what skills and traits hiring managers want to see.
  • Identify what keeps them engaged — and what pushes them away.
  • Learn how they evaluate portfolios and spot the best ones.

📚 Literature Review

  • Most spend less than 3 minutes reviewing a portfolio.
  • They expect 3–4 strong projects with full process visibility.
  • Storytelling and clear rationale matter more than flashy visuals.
  • Cluttered or confusing layouts can be a dealbreaker.

💼 Industry Analysis

  • Make info scannable with strong visual hierarchy.
  • Find the right balance between narrative and visuals.
  • Include rationale alongside the design — not buried in text.

Main Themes Extracted from Hiring manager interviews:

...
  • “I want to see how they think — not just what they made.”
  • “A case study that clearly shows the why behind each step is 10x more impressive than a flashy UI.”
  • “Don’t make me work hard to find your best work — it should be obvious.”

PHASE 2 👤 DEFINE

🧭 Who are we designing for?

While this project is technically about my portfolio, the real users aren't me — it's the people reviewing it under time pressure, making quick-fit decisions:

  • UX Hiring Managers
  • Design Leads
  • Recruiters

Through interviews, lit reviews, and industry analysis, I found that portfolio reviewers usually spend less than 2–5 minutes on a first pass. That means I need to communicate clearly and quickly — but still allow depth for those who want to explore.

✅ Usability Testing & Key Findings

To validate the high-fidelity prototype of my portfolio, I conducted a usability test with peers and industry professionals who had experience reviewing portfolios. The goal was to assess clarity, cognitive load, and navigational flow based on real user feedback.

Usability Testing Prototype

🔍 Testing Goals

  • Assess overall clarity and content hierarchy of key sections
  • Identify cognitive load issues
  • Evaluate navigation (including jump links vs. separate pages)
  • Understand which areas felt redundant or unnecessary

    • 🧠 UX Principles Guiding the Design

    Informed by usability tests, I applied two key principles that directly address recruiter/hiring manager behavior:

    1. Cognitive Load Reduction

    • Visually scannable
    • Clear in hierarchy
    • Predictable in navigation
    • Light on jargon or filler

    2. Progressive Disclosure

    • Case study summaries show key outcomes, tags, and roles — click to expand full story
    • Process sections are chunked with headings, visual cues, and expandable modules
    • The About section leads with a short pitch, followed by deeper content (philosophy, timeline, resume)

    🧠 Key Feedback & Insights

    📍 About Section

    Feedback: Users liked the concise storytelling and visuals, but found the experience timeline redundant since it’s already in the resume.

    Action Taken: Focused this section purely on narrative and personal brand; removed detailed experience to reduce repetition.

    📍 Projects Section

    Feedback: Project cards looked too dense and made quick browsing harder.

    Action Taken: Implemented progressive disclosure using hover effects to keep cards clean and reduce cognitive load.

    📍 Contact Section

    Feedback: A separate contact page disrupted flow and added an extra click.

    Action Taken: Integrated the contact form into the homepage and added jump links in the navbar for smoother access.


    PHASE-4 🚀 DELIVER

    Final Implementation & Key Improvements

    🏠 Home Screen

    The goal of the home section is simple—tell people who I am and what I do without making them scroll or guess.

    I kept the intro short and added a clear button to download my resume. This way, recruiters or hiring managers can get what they need right away.

    Earlier, I had the Home button placed beside the logo, assuming users would click the logo to return to the homepage. But during testing, it was clear that wasn’t working well. So I moved the Home link to the main nav with the rest, where it’s easier to find.


    💬 Rethinking the About Section

    I stripped out the traditional resume-style timeline, certifications, and testimonials.

    Instead, I focused on personal storytelling—sharing my journey, values, and design philosophy through a human-first lens. This helped create a more authentic connection with recruiters and reflected my approach to user-centered design.

    ✦ Goal: Let who I am shine through, not just what I’ve done.


    🗂️ Streamlining the Projects Section

    The original project cards had too much visible text, making them hard to scan and overwhelming at a glance.

    I simplified each card to show just the title, tags, and a clear CTA. Detailed information now appears only on hover using progressive disclosure.

    ✦ Result: Cleaner UI, reduced cognitive load, and a smoother browsing experience.


    📬 Rethinking the Contact Experience

    Originally, the contact form lived on a separate page — creating an awkward break in flow when users wanted to reach out.

    To make communication seamless, I embedded the form directly into the homepage (just above the footer) and added a navigation jump link. Now, visitors can get in touch with just one scroll or click — no extra page, no friction.

    ✦ Result: Reduced bounce rate on the contact page and aligned with modern user flow expectations.


    📈 Outcome

    • Built and deployed a responsive portfolio website using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, hosted via Netlify with version control maintained through GitHub.
    • Implemented clean and maintainable code architecture to ensure scalability and ease of updates.
    • Improved site navigation and content structure to reduce cognitive load — jump links were introduced for quicker access to key sections like Projects and Contact.
    • Applied principles of progressive disclosure to simplify project cards and improve content scanning.

    ✅ Results (So Far)

    This project is still a work in progress. What you see currently is a mid- to high-fidelity layout shaped by desk research, usability principles, and direct feedback from hiring managers.

    The visual structure and flow aim to reduce cognitive load and align more closely with what recruiters actually look for in UX portfolios. While the foundation is strong, there's still more to refine — including real content updates, detailed case study integration, and interaction enhancements.

    Next steps include usability testing on the updated version and iterating based on live feedback from target users (recruiters and hiring teams).
