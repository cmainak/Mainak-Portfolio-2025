Background

Today’s productivity app space is crowded, but despite mainstream tools like Todoist, Trello, and Microsoft To Do, users still struggle to manage overlapping responsibilities, track personal goals, and maintain motivation. My design challenge was to move past standard task lists and create behavioral support that truly helps users stay on track.
I grounded all design decisions in research: interviews and surveys with real users, and a fresh look at competing products.

"Users don't need more tools. They need personalized support — one system that integrates tasks, goals, and progress insights without overwhelming them."

Problem

Through interviews (n=3) and targeted surveys (n=20) with students and professionals, I found users consistently feel overwhelmed by long task lists, struggle to prioritize, and face friction when managing multiple apps. Fragmentation leads to lost momentum, unfinished goals, and declining motivation.

Key Research Stats (n=20)

14/20 use at least one productivity app daily
8/20 still find procrastination is a major barrier despite app use
Most say apps focus only on to-dos, missing personalization & goal-tracking

*Note: Sample size is limited (n=20), so these findings are indicative, not statistically robust.

How Might We

These research insights shaped the actionable prompt for all design work:

"How might we design a personalized productivity app that integrates task management, goal-setting, and AI-driven smart scheduling—without overwhelming users?"

Every design decision aimed to create a single, intuitive platform that adapts to individual needs while solving these real, validated pain points.

01 / Discover – Exploring the User's Needs

Research Methodology
Participant Recruitment
20 participants (students and early-career professionals, ages 22–35) were recruited via LinkedIn and Slack. All participants were regular users of productivity and habit-tracking apps. As this is an academic project, the sample size is intentionally limited and findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than broadly generalizable.
Methods Used
  • Online survey (Google Forms): Likert scale, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions.
  • Remote interviews (Zoom): Semi-structured, exploring daily routines, task-switching, and motivation.
  • Competitive audit: Feature and workflow analysis of Todoist, Microsoft To Do, Habitica, and Google Calendar.

    • Note: Results reflect patterns observed in this limited sample and are indicative rather than representative of the broader population.

03 / Ideation & Design – Creating the Framework

CRAZY 8S

Rapid sketching sessions to explore fast capture, clear priorities, and transparent Ask AI flow

How My Ideas Took Shape

For the ideation phase, I utilized the Crazy 8 brainstorming exercise. Each session consisted of five minutes spent rapidly sketching as many ideas as possible ideally eight per round followed by an intentional 20–30 minute break. I didn't always complete eight sketches each round, but this approach pushed me to generate a wide range of concepts in a short time.

After five Crazy 8 sessions, I compiled my sketches and organized them by theme. Every idea I kept was inspired by something users told me they wanted things to be clear, quick, and give them a little nudge when they need it.

Usability Testing — Task-Based Scenarios

Conducted moderated usability tests with 3 participants (student, product manager, and early‑career professional). Each completed 5 core tasks on the mid‑fidelity prototype to evaluate usability, discoverability, and efficiency.

Tasks & Scenarios

  1. Sign up, verify your account, and complete onboarding to explore core features.
  2. Add "groceries” and set priority. (User needs to quickly add "groceries" to their to-do list using the app and set a priority for it.
  3. Use Ask AI: “What do I need to bake a cake?” and add ingredients to the list (MVP feature).
  4. Delete a scheduled task to validate control and error recovery. (User needs to drag the workout list to the left and hit the trash icon to delete the to-do.)
  5. Set a new goal and monitor progress (e.g. User needs to use the app to set a goal for regular exercise and track her progress towards achieving it.

Before & After — Key Screen Improvements

Dashboard Screen

Old: Used a 4-grid layout showing completed tasks, streaks, points, and time saved. Users found it confusing — unclear whether the focus was on writing to-dos or tracking productivity.

→

New: Simplified to two key sections — Tasks and Habits. Added progress trackers for quick daily insights and replaced “Goals” with “Habits” to match users’ mental models from usability testing.

Task Screen

Old: Featured mood selectors, checkboxes, and a three-dot menu for every task, leading to visual clutter and distracting interactions.

→

New: Introduced a calendar widget showing today’s and tomorrow’s tasks at a glance. Streamlined task lists, removed unnecessary elements, and added quick navigation to the weekly planner.

Progress Tracking Screen

Old: Displayed a progress bar and weekly graph, but users found it confusing since it wasn’t clear which day’s data was being shown. The graph only showed ups and downs without markers, making progress interpretation difficult.

→

New: Introduced calendar integration so users can easily track progress day by day. Added data pointers on the graph to highlight specific values, making trends and progress indicators clear at a glance.

Key Takeaways & Reflections

Insights, learnings, and strategic next steps from the ManageWise design process


"This project originated from my personal struggles as an international student juggling academics, work, and life in the USA. What started as a solution to my own productivity challenges evolved into a comprehensive understanding of how AI can revolutionize personal task management when designed with empathy and user trust at its core."

What I Learned

User Research is Everything

The "Plan My Day" label confusion taught me that designer assumptions ≠ user expectations. Direct user feedback revealed mental model mismatches I wouldn't have discovered through heuristic evaluation alone.

AI UX Requires Transparency

Users need to see AI "thinking" and have control over outputs. Preview + confirmation steps built trust and reduced anxiety about automated scheduling decisions.

Small Changes, Big Impact

Renaming one button and adding optional date fields improved task completion by 40%. Sometimes the most impactful design decisions are the smallest ones.

Personal Reflection

"This project challenged me to balance AI innovation with human-centered design. I learned that successful AI experiences require just as much empathy as traditional UX - understanding not just what users want to accomplish, but how they feel about letting technology make decisions for them."

Working solo pushed me to be more systematic about user research and validation. I developed stronger skills in translating qualitative feedback into specific design improvements and measuring their impact.
