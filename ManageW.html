Personalizing Productivity Through AI

ROLE: UX Researcher & Product Designer

TIMELINE: 10 Weeks

TOOLS: Figma, FigJam, Google Forms, Notion

TEAM: Solo (peer testers)

SCOPE: Concept-to-prototype of a productivity app with AI-assisted scheduling and goals

This project is a fictitious scenario, completed as a part of a graduate program. View High-Fidelity Prototype

Background

The productivity app space is crowded — Todoist, Trello, Microsoft To Do — yet users still struggle to manage overlapping tasks, track personal goals, and stay motivated.

“Users don’t need more tools. They need personalized support — one system that integrates tasks, goals, and progress insights without overwhelming them.”

Problem

Individuals feel overwhelmed by long task lists, struggle to prioritize, and juggle multiple tools (calendars, notes, to-dos), which fragments planning and reduces follow-through.

Key Research Stats:

  • 70% of participants use at least one productivity app daily.
  • 40% still struggle with procrastination despite using these tools.
  • Most existing apps focus on tasks only, ignoring personalization and integrated goal-tracking.

How Might We

“How might we design a personalized productivity app that integrates task management, goal-setting, and AI-driven smart scheduling — without overwhelming users?”

This question guided the design process, focusing on creating a single, intuitive platform that adapts to each user’s unique needs and preferences.

Affinity mapping

Inputs: 3 interviews - 2 surveys (n=10 + n=10) - competitor scan
Purpose

We synthesized interview notes, survey comments, and competitor insights into clusters to reveal shared pain points and prioritize opportunities for the first iteration.

Process

3 interviews, 2 short surveys (n=10 + n=10), competitor scan (Todoist, Microsoft To Do, Trello). We grouped observations into themes and mapped each theme to a concrete design move.

Limitation: directional insights from small, convenience samples—used to shape early prototypes, not to generalize.

Competitor resonance: Todoist robust yet overwhelming; MS To Do simple but limited customization—reinforcing low‑friction capture, clear priorities, and integrated scheduling.

03 / Ideate – Creating the Framework

How My Ideas Took Shape

For the ideation phase, I utilized the Crazy 8 brainstorming exercise. Each session consisted of five minutes spent rapidly sketching as many ideas as possible—ideally eight per round—followed by an intentional 20–30 minute break. I didn’t always complete eight sketches each round, but this approach pushed me to generate a wide range of concepts in a short time and gave me space to reflect between sessions.

After five Crazy 8 sessions, I compiled my sketches and organized them by theme. I filled pages with ideas, doodles, and quick notes. Then, I circled the ones that made me think, “Yes, this actually helps!” Every idea I kept was inspired by something users told me—they wanted things to be clear, quick, and give them a little nudge when they need it.

Pulling everything together, I mapped a complete user flow for Surplus Manager. 04 / Prototype – Let's Make the Design!

Wireframes

While developing the storyboard concept of paying by paycheck, I examined Bank of America’s mobile banking app and noticed features like “Bill Pay” and “Life Plan” that had been available for years but were largely unnoticed by users. Even participants who were existing customers had never used them.

I decided to integrate similar ideas into my finance management feature. Participants managed finances based on their paycheck, bills, and savings goals, so the new feature would allow them to set up their plan based on pay schedules (bi-weekly, monthly, or custom).

The “Money Manager” feature appears on the dashboard and displays the available balance, scheduled bill payments, and remaining funds until the next paycheck. I also integrated goal tracking and easy access to upcoming payments.

Low-Fidelity Prototype Usability Testing

I tested this prototype with 3 participants. Feedback revealed that some UI elements caused confusion, such as large negative balances in “Pending Transactions.” Based on insights, I iterated the flow to prioritize surplus balance management rather than individual payment flows.

High-Fidelity Prototype

Based on user feedback, I renamed the feature from “Money Manager” to “Surplus Manager” to better reflect its purpose. Participants found the previous name misleading as it sounded investment-related.

Key Highlights

  • Manage by Pay Period: Users can track spending across pay cycles.

05 / Test – It’s Time to Test the Prototype!

Usability Test Plan

I conducted tests with 4 participants—2 of whom were part of the low-fidelity prototype testing. For high-fidelity testing, the platform did not allow Bank of America demo logins, so participants used screenshots and scenario-based tasks. I ensured the interface felt intuitive for both Bank of America users and non-users.

Usability Test Findings

Participants appreciated the feature, noting it would simplify financial tracking and make the experience engaging. Key feedback included:

  • “Select Amount” confused users—they thought it referred to paying from the Surplus Balance.
  • One participant assumed “Minimum Amount” meant “total due” rather than minimum payment.
  • The term “Open Balances” was unclear for some participants, but they liked the interactive chart after explanation.
  • Participants suggested integrating Outgoing Payments more closely with outstanding balance management.

Priority Revisions

Based on feedback, I created a new user flow focusing on the Outgoing Payments pathway. This approach offers a more direct route for paying down debt and provides better clarity during transactions.
