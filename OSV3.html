Chapter 01 — Discover

Why simple grocery decisions
feel so hard

Where this started: This project started in a grocery aisle. I watched a friend with lactose intolerance read a label, look up unfamiliar ingredients, and still leave unsure if the product was safe. It made me realize that giving people a lot of information doesn't help if it isn't easy to understand.

65%

of US shoppers struggle to interpret food labels

72%

want clearer ingredient information (FDA 2024–25)

4 Pain Points That Surfaced

1

Confusing Labels

Technical ingredient names and vague health claims create uncertainty at the shelf.

2

Hidden Additives

Shoppers Google unfamiliar ingredients mid-aisle, wasting time and adding stress.

3

Dietary Restrictions

No easy way to verify if plant-based or dairy-free claims are trustworthy.

4

Decision Fatigue

Comparing multiple products leads to analysis paralysis and suboptimal choices.

Validating the Problem

A single story is a great starting point, but I needed to know if this was a widespread problem. To find out, I ran a 22-person survey, conducted four interviews, and observed shoppers in-store. My goal wasn't just to validate an idea, but to truly understand how people make food choices when they're in a hurry.

The Business Opportunity: Finding the Gap

While evaluating the market, a clear business opportunity emerged. Competitors provided data, but hid actionable personalization—like allergy filters—behind paywalls. Key Insight: The opportunity was not to overwhelm users with more data, but to offer immediate, trustworthy guidance as a core, un-paywalled feature.

Feature Yuka Fooducate Open Food Facts
Personalized allergy filters ✗ Paywall ✗
Alternative product suggestions ✓ Partial ✗
Reward / loyalty system ✗ ✗ ✗
U.S. FDA regulatory alignment Limited Partial Data only

Opportunity Gap → The strongest opportunity sat at the intersection of personalization and trust. Existing tools either buried these capabilities behind paywalls or treated them as secondary concerns.

The Core Challenge

How might we translate complex food labels into instant, personalized insights so shoppers can make confident decisions in seconds?

System Architecture: Heavy Lifting in Under 2 Seconds

1. Scan → 2. Instant Health Score + Breakdown → 3. Personalized Swap Suggestions

Early Ideation

Minimum Viable Loop: Scan → Immediate health score + ingredient breakdown → Personalized swap suggestions. Drafted into mid-fidelity wireframes and tested with 4 target users (20–30 min sessions) before high-fidelity design.

Usability Testing Revealed 3 Major Speed Bumps

Remember Stephanie? She needs instant, binary answers to get out of the aisle quickly. But during testing, our initial designs forced her to do mental math and wait 30 seconds for a separate loading screen. We had to fix these critical friction points.

! Critical

30-Second Wait

"Why do I land on a blank camera screen first?"

Fix → Rating now overlays the camera in <2s via progressive disclosure.

⚠ Caution

Score Hard to Parse

"I see 41/100, is that good or bad?"

Fix → Color-coded badge + traffic-light bar showing safe/risky zones.

✓ Resolved

Toggles Felt Tedious

"Too many switches, then I still have to hit Done."

Fix → Icon grid replacing the dense toggle list, with a bottom anchored Save button.

Turning Insights Into Solutions

D1

Overlay vs. Separate Screen

In my first wireframes, the results loaded on a separate screen after scanning. It felt natural scan, then read. But when I tested this with participants, users waited up to 30 seconds to get an answer. One asked, "Why do I land on a blank camera screen first?" The product was designed around my mental model, not theirs. I rebuilt the scanner around progressive disclosure: the health rating appears as an overlay on the camera feed in under two seconds. Full details are one swipe away. The change felt small. The impact wasn't.

Before →

A numerical score without context asks users to do mental math they shouldn't have to do at a grocery shelf.

After ✓

Color coding plus plain-language risk tags turns a decision into a glance. No interpretation required.

D2

Traffic Light vs. Numerical Score

My original prototype used a numerical score for example, a product might receive a 41/100. In testing, nobody knew what that score actually meant.

⚕️ Aligned with FDA research on color-coded labeling reducing cognitive load.

Before →

A numerical score without context asks users to do mental math they shouldn't have to do at a grocery shelf.

After ✓

Color coding plus plain-language risk tags turns a decision into a glance. No interpretation required.

D3

List & Toggles vs. Visual Icon Grid

Constraint: The original screen presented preferences as a plain checkbox list functional but visually cold and mentally taxing.

Decision: Replaced with a grid of branded icon chips. Each preference gets a custom icon + label, making the screen scannable at a glance. A bottom anchored Save button replaced the top nav "Done" action, consistent with iOS thumb zones.

Result: Users no longer read line by line. They recognize preferences visually, while the Save button gives cleaner hierarchy.

Before →

Dense toggle list users had to read each label individually.

After ✓

Icon-first grid: instantly scannable, visually distinct, thumb friendly Save CTA.

Lessons Learned

Testing the early prototypes completely broke my own assumptions. I originally designed flows around how I thought apps "should" work like moving a user to a new screen after scanning. Watching users stumble over those decisions taught me that surface level logic often fails in practice. It reinforced my drive to prototype and test in realistic environments as early as possible, rather than waiting for a polished, but flawed, final product.
