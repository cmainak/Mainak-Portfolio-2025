Chapter 01 — Discover

Why simple grocery decisions
feel so hard

Where this started: This project did not begin with a market gap spreadsheet. It began in a grocery aisle. I watched a friend with lactose intolerance scan a carton, search unfamiliar ingredients, and still leave unsure whether the product was safe. The issue was not a lack of information. It was a lack of clarity.

65%

of US shoppers struggle to interpret food labels

72%

want clearer ingredient information (FDA 2024–25)

4 Pain Points That Surfaced Repeatedly

1

Confusing Labels

Technical ingredient names and vague health claims create uncertainty at the shelf.

2

Hidden Additives

Shoppers Google unfamiliar ingredients mid-aisle, wasting time and adding stress.

3

Dietary Restrictions

No easy way to verify if plant-based or dairy-free claims are trustworthy.

4

Decision Fatigue

Comparing multiple products leads to analysis paralysis and suboptimal choices.

How I Validated This at Scale

One story is compelling but not enough to design around. I wanted to understand whether this frustration was isolated or systemic. That led to a 22-person survey, followed by four interviews and in-store observation sessions. The goal was less about validating an idea and more about understanding how people actually make food decisions under time pressure.

Finding the Gap in a Crowded Market

The competitive landscape was crowded, but the overlap was incomplete. Most products solved isolated problems while leaving the broader decision experience fragmented.

Key Insight: People were not asking for more nutritional data. They were asking for confidence. The opportunity was not to overwhelm users with information, but to translate complexity into trustworthy guidance.

Feature Yuka Fooducate Open Food Facts
Personalized allergy filters ✗ Paywall ✗
Alternative product suggestions ✓ Partial ✗
Reward / loyalty system ✗ ✗ ✗
U.S. FDA regulatory alignment Limited Partial Data only

Opportunity Gap → The strongest opportunity sat at the intersection of personalization and trust. Existing tools either buried these capabilities behind paywalls or treated them as secondary concerns.

Chapter 03 — Develop

Ideation, Testing &
Key Decisions

Minimum Viable Loop: Scan → Immediate health score + ingredient breakdown → Personalized swap suggestions. Drafted into mid-fidelity wireframes and tested with 4 target users (20–30 min sessions) before high-fidelity design.

Core User Flow

System Architecture: Heavy Lifting in Under 2 Seconds

The entire experience is architected around a single constraint: minimum steps, maximum clarity. Once a barcode is scanned, the system runs health analysis, scoring, ingredient breakdown, and risk flagging simultaneously.

1. Scan → 2. Instant Health Score + Breakdown → 3. Personalized Swap Suggestions

Early Ideation: Breadth Over Perfection

Before committing to visual design, I generated dozens of mid-fidelity wireframes to explore layout, hierarchy, and flow — mapping the entire experience without getting bogged down in aesthetics.

Usability Testing Revealed 3 Major Speed Bumps

Moderated sessions with 4 target users (20–30 min each). Tasks: scan product, read rating, save food preferences.

⚠ Caution

Score Hard to Parse

"I see 41/100, is that good or bad?"

Fix → Color-coded badge + traffic-light bar showing safe/risky zones + High/Limited risk tags.

! Critical

30-Second Wait

"Why do I land on a blank camera screen first?"

Fix → Rating now overlays the camera in <2s via progressive disclosure. Full details on swipe-up sheet.

✓ Resolved

Toggles Felt Tedious

"Too many switches, then I still have to hit Done."

Fix → Visual icon grid replacing the dense toggle list, with a bottom-anchored Save button.

Key Decisions & Critical Trade-offs

D1

Overlay vs. Separate Screen

Constraint: Users waited up to 30 seconds for a separate screen to load after scanning.
Decision: Shifted to an instant overlay with progressive disclosure.
Result: Perceived wait time reduced to <2s. Users could see pass/fail before going to the detailed screen.

Before →

Camera opens → blank screen → wait 30s for product details.

After ✓

Rating appears as overlay in <2s. Full details via swipe-up sheet.

D2

Traffic Light vs. Numerical Score

Constraint: A score of "41/100" lacked context.
Decision: Replace with traffic-light system + risk tags vs. adding explanatory text.
Result: 60% improvement in comprehension. Validated by FDA research on color-coded labeling.

Before →

Raw numbers and basic lists required mental math.

After ✓

Traffic-light color coding + risk tags for instant comprehension.

D3

List & Toggles vs. Visual Icon Grid

Constraint: The original screen presented preferences as a plain checkbox list — functional but visually cold and mentally taxing.
Decision: Replaced with a grid of branded icon chips. Each preference gets a custom icon + label, making the screen scannable at a glance. A bottom-anchored Save button replaced the top nav "Done" action, consistent with iOS thumb zones.
Result: Users no longer read line by line. They recognize preferences visually, while the Save button gives cleaner hierarchy.

Before →

Dense toggle list — users had to read each label individually.

After ✓

Icon-first grid: instantly scannable, visually distinct, thumb-friendly Save CTA.

Lessons Learned

This research experience deeply shaped how I approach design. Listening to real stories taught me that surface-level observations often miss the most meaningful insights. It strengthened my desire to always dig deeper — to uncover root causes behind user behaviors and emotions, not just visible pain points.
