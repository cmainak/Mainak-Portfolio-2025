01 / Discover

Why simple grocery decisions feel so hard

The problem started by watching a friend with lactose intolerance scan a plant-based milk carton for 2 minutes, Googling ingredients mid-aisle, and still leaving unsure if it was safe.

65% of U.S. shoppers report difficulty interpreting food labels. 72% want clearer ingredient information. The FDA emphasized clearer nutrition labeling in 2024–25, validating the need at both regulatory and consumer levels.

1

Confusing Labels

Technical ingredient names and vague health claims create uncertainty at the shelf.

2

Hidden Additives

Shoppers Google unfamiliar ingredients mid-aisle, wasting time and adding stress.

3

Dietary Restrictions

No easy way to verify if plant-based or dairy-free claims are trustworthy without deep label reading.

4

Decision Fatigue

Comparing multiple products leads to analysis paralysis and suboptimal choices.

How I validated this at scale

To move beyond a single anecdote, I ran a 22-person survey (ages 19–52, 40% with dietary restrictions) recruited via Reddit nutrition forums and campus boards, followed by 4 semi-structured interviews with in-store shadowing, including a session at a Trader Joe’s dairy aisle. I then ran a competitive audit of Yuka, Fooducate, and Open Food Facts, and reviewed FDA and EU nutrition label guidelines.

03 / Develop

Ideation, Testing & Key Decisions

Minimum Viable Loop: Scan → Immediate health score + ingredient breakdown → Personalized swap suggestions. Drafted into mid-fidelity wireframes and tested with 4 target users (20–30 min sessions) before high-fidelity design.

System Architecture

Doing the heavy lifting in under 2 seconds

The entire experience is architected around a single constraint: minimum steps, maximum clarity. Once a barcode is scanned, the system runs health analysis, score calculation, ingredient breakdown, and risk flagging simultaneously.

1. Scan → 2. Instant Health Score + Breakdown → 3. Personalized Swap Suggestions
Early Ideation

Exploring breadth over perfection in early ideation

Before committing to visual design, I generated dozens of mid-fidelity wireframes to explore layout, hierarchy, and flow. The goal was to map the entire experience without getting bogged down in aesthetics.

Usability testing revealed three major speed bumps

Moderated sessions with 4 target users (20–30 min each). Tasks: scan product, read rating, save food preferences.

Issue: Score hard to parse

"I see 41/100, is that good or bad?"

Added color-coded badge + traffic-light bar showing safe/risky zones + High/Limited risk tags.

Issue: 30-second wait after scan

"Why do I land on a blank camera screen first?"

Rating now overlays the camera in <2s via progressive disclosure. Full details on swipe-up sheet.

Issue: Toggles felt tedious

"Too many switches, then I still have to hit Done."

Replaced the dense toggle list with a visual icon grid, making preferences easier to scan, recognize, and select. A bottom-anchored Save button creates a clearer and more polished completion point.

Key Decisions & Critical Trade-offs

Decision 1: Overlay vs. Separate Screen

Constraint: Users waited up to 30 seconds for a separate screen to load after scanning.
Decision: Shifted to an instant overlay section with progressive disclosure.
Result: Perceived wait time reduced to <2s. Users could see pass/fail before going to the detailed screen.

Before: Camera opens → blank screen → wait 30s for product details.

→

After: Rating appears as an overlay in under 2s. Full details via swipe-up sheet.

Decision 2: Traffic Light vs. Numerical Score

Constraint: A score of "41/100" lacked context.
Trade-off: Add explanatory text vs. replace with traffic-light system + risk tags.
Result: 60% improvement in comprehension. Validated by FDA research on color-coded labeling.

Before: Raw numbers and basic lists required mental math.

→

After: Traffic-light color coding + risk tags for instant comprehension.

Decision 3: List & Toggles vs. Visual Icon Grid

Constraint: The original screen presented preferences as a plain checkbox list with toggle switches functional but visually cold and mentally taxing. Each item looked identical, forcing users to read every label before making a choice.
Decision: Replaced the text-only list with a grid of branded icon chips; each preference gets a custom illustrated icon and a label, making the entire screen scannable at a glance. The "Done" top nav action was replaced with a single prominent Save button anchored at the bottom, consistent with iOS native patterns and thumb-reachable interaction zones.
Result: Users no longer read line by line to make choices. They recognize preferences visually, while the anchored Save button gives the screen a cleaner hierarchy and a more intentional finish.

Before: A dense toggle list with identical rows users had to read each label individually to build their preferences.

→

After: Icon-first grid design instantly scannable, visually distinct, with a thumb-friendly Save CTA anchored at the bottom.

Lessons Learned

This research experience deeply shaped the way I approach design. Listening to real stories taught me that surface-level observations often miss the most meaningful insights. It strengthened my desire to always dig deeper to uncover root causes behind user behaviors and emotions, not just visible pain points.

What comes next

  2. Expand the design system component library.
  3. Conduct a longitudinal study to measure actual behavior change over a 30-day period.
  4. Focus on validating the scoring model with nutrition experts and expanding into a reusable component library for future features.
