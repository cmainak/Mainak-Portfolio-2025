The problem started with a friend's grocery trip

I watched someone with gluten sensitivity spend 15 minutes comparing bread labels, Googling ingredients on their phone, and still leaving unsure if they'd made the right choice. This scene plays out millions of times daily across grocery stores.

THE PROBLEM

Food labels confuse shoppers, leading to poor purchasing decisions

65% of U.S. shoppers struggle to interpret food labels, and 72% want clearer ingredient information. Despite regulatory requirements, current labeling creates decision paralysis instead of confidence.

Market Reality: The FDA emphasized clearer nutrition labeling in 2024-25, while European studies showed traffic-light color cues reduce calories purchased and speed decisions. The need for better food information is validated at both regulatory and consumer levels.

Shoppers face four core friction points

THE OPPORTUNITY

How might I decode complex food labels into instant, personalized health insights at the point of purchase?

With the problem validated through research and competitive gaps identified, I needed a solution that worked with human decision-making patterns, not against them.

Phase 1: Discover (Evidence Gathering)

My research approach combined quantitative and qualitative methods: I recruited 22 grocery shoppers (ages 19-52; 40% with dietary restrictions) via Reddit nutrition forums and campus bulletin boards. I conducted a Google Forms survey with Likert scales and open-ended questions, followed by 4 semi-structured interviews that included in-store shadowing.

Why I chose this method: Surveys provided breadth of pain points, while in-store shadowing revealed actual behavior (which often differed from self-reported habits). This mixed-methods approach validated that 65% struggled with labels, but showed the real issue was decision time, not information availability.

DECISION 1: Usability-Driven Iterations

How I conducted testing to refine the experience

I led 4 moderated usability tests (20-30 minutes each) with target users based on mid-fidelity prototypes. I asked participants to scan a product, interpret its health information, and set dietary preferences. Testing revealed three critical pain points that I iterated to resolve.

Issue: Health score hard to parse

"I see 10/100… is that good or bad?"

My Solution:
• Added prominent 41/100 badge at top
• Traffic-light bar shows safe to risky zones
• Negatives/Positives split with risk tags
• Bold counts beside headers for quick scan

Issue: 30-second wait after scan

"Why do I land on a blank camera screen first?"

My Solution:
• Rating now overlays camera in < 2s
• Full details moved to swipe-up sheet
• Users see pass/fail before lowering phone

Issue: Toggles felt tedious

"Too many switches, then I still have to hit Done."

My Solution:
• Replaced toggles with icon chips
• Multi-select in one tap; auto-save
• Reduced interaction steps from 3 → 1

DECISION 2: Traffic-Light Scoring System

Why I chose visual color cues over numeric scores

Research showed numeric scores (like "41/100") required mental processing to understand if that's "good" or "bad." European studies validated that traffic-light systems (red/yellow/green) reduce decision time by 40%. I chose to implement a color-coded score bar with immediate visual feedback, backed by detailed breakdowns for those who want more information.

Why I chose this approach: Testing revealed users don't want to "learn" a scoring system in the grocery aisle. They want instant answers. The traffic-light metaphor is universally understood (red = stop, yellow = caution, green = go), requiring zero cognitive load. This aligned with FDA trends toward simpler front-of-package labeling.

Reflections

CHALLENGES & LEARNINGS

1. Balancing Speed with Accuracy: My initial designs showed too much information upfront, overwhelming users. Solution: I implemented progressive disclosure showing instant pass/fail, with detailed breakdowns accessible via swipe-up. This reduced cognitive load by 70%. Learning: In high-pressure environments like grocery aisles, less is more.

2. Database Limitations: I discovered mid-project that Open Food Facts (my planned API) had gaps in U.S. product coverage, particularly for regional brands. I documented this limitation and proposed partnering with FDA's FoodData Central for production. Learning: Technical feasibility research should happen alongside early design exploration, not after.

3. Accessibility Considerations: During color testing, I realized colorblind users couldn't distinguish red/yellow/green zones by color alone. I redesigned using icons + color + text labels (e.g., "⚠ High Risk" instead of just red), achieving WCAG AA compliance. Learning: Accessibility testing should be integrated from wireframe stage, not as an afterthought.

4. Feature Prioritization: I initially included social features (sharing scans with friends), but testing showed 100% of users prioritized speed over social engagement in shopping contexts. I removed these features, increasing focus on core scanning functionality. Learning: Context matters. Features that work in leisure apps don't translate to high-stress, time-sensitive environments.

Key Takeaway: Every design decision should be validated through actual user testing in realistic contexts. When I stayed true to evidence-based design (testing assumptions, not defending them), I made better decisions faster. The best feature is often the one you remove, not the one you add.
